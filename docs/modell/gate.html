Cannibalization model · version 4

The gate between the glut and the demand that drains it

Version 3 drew flexible demand as responding directly to the glut. It does not. It responds to the glut relative to a breakeven, and that breakeven is set by three policy terms: electricity tax, network tariff, and the carbon price on the fossil alternative. Making the gate explicit is what changed in this version, and it is where the July 2026 EU package acts.

The four loops, with the breakeven gate made explicit

The demand fork is not symmetric

Change 1 · the fork is not symmetric

Electrified industrial heat carries roughly 600 TWh a year of EU technical potential against data centres at about 70 TWh today and 115 TWh by 2030. The two arms differ by a factor of five in size before any argument about jobs per kWh.

For scale at the other end: Finland's entire electric-boiler district-heat output, the result that proves the mechanism, is 2.6 TWh. That is 0.4% of the industrial heat prize.

Change 2 · flat load is not flexible load

This revises the v3 claim that data centres absorb the glut equally. They do not, because their load is close to flat. Flat demand raises the price level in all hours roughly alike; it does not fill the midday trough. Capture rate depends on the trough, so per MWh a flexible load shaped to surplus hours does far more for B1 than a firm load of the same size.

Data centres therefore enter this diagram mainly through the crowding-out arrow, drawn heavy, rather than as a co-equal claimant on the glut.

Change 3 · the gate, and what July 2026 did to it

The breakeven near €54/MWh assumes an installation paying close to the full carbon price: a gas boiler emits about 0.22 tCO2 per MWh of heat, which at €70 a tonne is the €16/MWh in the gas-side cost. An installation on free allocation faces an effective price nearer €10, so its gas steam costs about €46 and the electric breakeven no longer clears.

The 17 July package cuts the tax-and-tariff term and weakens the carbon term at once, substituting an Industrial Decarbonisation Bank and €4bn of allowances for the price signal.

Where the carbon price actually bites

Moving the threshold from €54 to €40 costs SE1 and SE2 about 3 points of qualifying hours and costs SE3 thirteen and SE4 twelve. Where power is already very cheap the carbon price is nearly irrelevant; where it is marginal the carbon price is close to decisive.

Weakening it does not cancel electrification, it strips out the middle of the distribution, which is where most of Europe's industry sits.

What did not change

B1, B2 and R1 keep their v3 structure and signs. The capture-rate mechanism, the flexibility exit and the deindustrialization spiral are unaltered; the gate is inserted between the glut and the exit rather than replacing anything.

The battery case remains B2 seen from the arbitrageur's revenue, with the addition that value migrates to duration: doubling NSW duration to 3.2 hours turned a 74% fall in $/MWh into a 49% fall in $/MW, while Queensland's flat duration lost 73% on both.

Read with care

The 600 TWh is technical potential, not a forecast, and the economically viable share today is far smaller and concentrated in the zones on the left of the price distribution. The data centre figures are demand projections on current policy and carry their own wide range, up to 287 TWh in the most aggressive published estimate.

The €10 effective carbon price is a reported average across freely-allocated operators, not a rate any single installation faces.
